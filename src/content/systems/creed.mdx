Building [Punchcard](https://punchcard.com), the AI-native platform for financial audits, [Nathan Turnbow](https://www.linkedin.com/in/turnbow/) and I found ourselves facing some constraints common to lean engineering teams. Large techincal surface area (document ingestion at scale, custom user workflows, chat agents, project managment UIs, etc), whith reliability as a non-negotiable for anyone to trust the product with live audits. An engineering team a 1-10% the size of established incumbents. And most of our time needed to go to actually understanding customers and trying a lot of approaches to solving novel problems before one stuck.

The idea for a horizontally scalable, internal engineering agent did not take long to germinate and sprout in my mind. The two ambitious targets being:

- No more user-reported bugs (zero!)
- A peer-level internal engineer who could be trusted withend-to-end ownership of finding bugs *and* fixing them, both proactively and reactively

That said, having worked with AI coding agents for a while, I knew the risks of creating an "AI-slop cannon" at scale was real:

- Heavy token burn for marginal insight
- Half-finished or shortsighted fixes
- Risky code around customer data and hot paths
- Accidentally optimizing for the wrong reward
- Maintenance, which is always easy to overlook

AI being involved in *fixing* bugs was obvious. Where did it fit on the QA side? Well — the same place a human does. Testing every permutation of any extensive product does not scale. You try to understand the highest-value, realistic user journeys through it.

The earliest version was dubbed "Exterminator". Not just another bug bot - the ultimatum on all bugs: ideally, none of them escape to prod and hit a real user. We later renamed it Creed, after the iconic head of QA at Dunder Mifflin. 

## Three loops, not one agent

Rather than a single orchestrator, Creed is a distributed graph of ~40 distinct actions (each a distinct role, incentive, and trigger), grouped core into three core self-contained loops.

![Map, Verify, and Fix share memory.](/images/systems/creed-loops.svg)

**Map** keeps a picture of the product truthful and turns it into missions. **Verify** walks those missions in a real browser, around the clock. **Fix** turns a verified finding into a merged, re-tested change.

Two other things feed the same fix queue. After a deploy, a review walk hits what just shipped. In production, errors and session friction show up on their own. Prevent and react, same system.

A nightly "Warden" is allowed to repair the factory, but never the product.

The jobs are throwaway. A cron tick, a label, or a deploy puts work on a queue. The worker writes a few artifacts — maps, missions, findings, labels — and exits. The next job reads those; it isn't called. If two walks finish at once, you re-read before you write so one doesn't blow away the other. The walks started by enqueueing themselves but the traffic got lumpy, and hard to manage against paced dollar costs.

This was before frontier companies started offering out-of-the-box cloud agents and cron jobs, so a lot of the rails + runner infra was hand-rolled through GitHub Actions and self-hosted on AWS. It was great to see those patterns started shipping out of the box.

## High signal, or it is spam

One tradeoff became very clear as I montiered the traffic for the first week. We could get a high volume of PRs — every possible fix — with a lot of noise mixed in (false positives, bandaids slapped on bigger issues, etc.), or a lower volume with extremely high merge confidence. High signal wins every time, otherwise it turns into an email inbox overflowed with messages that feel safe to ignore.

A system built for horizontal scale that still needs a developer in every step just meant Nathan and I were queuing ourselves up as full-time reviewers, and losing the R&D, and [becoming the bottleneck](https://linearb.io/dev-interrupted/podcast/linearb-2026-benchmarks-ai-pr-merge-rate).

Browser walks via Playwright can be flaky, and LLMs are, in my experience, pretty bad at scoring their own confidence like you get from a traditional neural net — so we treat a bug report as a binary: is this reproducible, or not?

## Don't reconstruct the story of Moby Dick from ship blueprints

In terms of maintaining a knowledge base, a naive approach is asking an llm to infer user journeys and product intent from static code analysis.

Fortunately, we already had an important building block to use. Self-maintining, user-facing product documentation, which had been built for the changelog and for [CoAudit](/systems/coaudit) — framed from a **user** perspective, derived from PR and commit descriptions (which coding agents have done wonders for), rather than static code analysis. That second one drifts very quickly into storytelling anchored in React component trees, which is not unlike trying to tell the story of Moby Dick by looking at Captain Ahab's whaling ship blueprints. 

Later we paired that with more structured maps: deterministic enough to plan a walk and seed the environment it needs, making it much cheaper to seed fixtures and determine the preprequisties before pointing an llm at testing a given user journey.

For a while nothing really owned that seeding — creating fixtures, keeping them honest, unblocking missions that were just stalled in staging because the data wasn't there. Someone had to pick that up without also being the one who makes the test pass by making the fixture unrealistic. That's the kind of gap I still opened a PR for. Not the bug. The missing owner.

## Nobody grades their own homework

<blockquote class="pull">
  <p>Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome.</p>
  <footer>
    <a href="https://fs.blog/bias-incentives-reinforcement/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Charlie Munger</a>
  </footer>
</blockquote>

If this thing was ever going to be unsupervised and long-running, it needed both the right incentives and no way to game them. Classic example: give it "drive reported bugs to zero" and it's not out of the question that it authors its own tests, or reviews its own code. [Frontier models will hack the reward](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/) if you leave a gap.

I never actually watched Creed do those things — I built it with that in mind from the get-go. What we *did* have to optimize against was auto-merge escape rate. Without a hard "no escapes that don't work," a review prompt gets friendlier in a hurry.

Enter adversarial review. After a month or two of me taking the burden of reviewing every PR, we had a labeled set. Then I ran something in the spirit of [Karpathy's autoresearch](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch) on the review prompt itself: champion/challenger, keep the version that auto-approves more of the good PRs, kill it if escape rate moves. Letting any reverted or closed-as-wrong PR from that set through was non-negotiable. Those were exactly the ones that had already proved incomplete.

A few rules that follow from that:

- The implementer is not the merger
- The merge rules are not read from the PR
- Fix PRs do not close issues — only a targeted re-test does
- The Warden cannot close findings on intake's behalf
- Its self-merge is path-gated to pipeline code; a second job fails the night if anything landed outside that list
- Product fixes it files stay draft for a human

Very important here: this software runs in live financial audits, under a lot of trust, with sensitive client data. Auth, migrations, infra, database mutations — they can run through every gate, but the last one is still a human.

We'd been building validators into the repo for a while — anything that can reject a change, not just the deterministic stuff. I first heard [Dex Horthy](https://www.humanlayer.dev/blog/context-efficient-backpressure) call that *backpressure*: keep the signals that bounce a change cheap enough that a long-running agent can actually use them. Lint and types, dead-code scans, unit tests, integration tests and proof-of-work, and adversarial review sitting in the same stack.

Proof-of-work sounds obvious, but it mattered. When PRs are coming in at high volume on a small team, median review time should drop as close to zero as you can get it. That means the highest-signal proofs first. A screenshot that the app actually compiled, that the full stack still works, and that the page didn't regress — three questions in one glance. Then you drill the known dangerous diffs.

Match the model to the job, too. The always-on walk is thorough, not a novel. Cheapest adequate model first; escalate when the job is harder. That's most of the efficiency story.

After that labeled month, median review time was supposed to go to zero — except those exits. A gated process that actually holds is what keeps your attention from getting sucked into code that could have been corrected autonomously.

## Something has to fix the factory

On a distributed system like this I knew there'd be paper cuts. Flaky infra. An unknown path for jobs to stall. Issue queues growing faster than the fix queue. A nightly agent looks at the north stars I actually care about — PRs merged, walks run, PRs reverted, queue health — and can author fixes to the rails. What it cannot do is rewrite the incentives it's scored on, or quietly merge a product change to make the graph look better.

[HumanLayer tried a lights-out factory and shut it down in months](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/context-engineering-with-dex-horthy) once nobody had read the code. We wanted hands-free, not unread. Those wins did not come cheap.

The important piece is that this is coverage that lets us both prevent *and* react. I don't have exact support-case numbers, but the shift has been noticeable enough to know something changed. Sure — confounding variables exist, models got better, etc. The proof-of-work and the ticket numbers are the parts I'd actually stand on. Anecdotally, I'd estimate we fielded close to a fifth of the bug reports while the customer base grew about 5× over the same period.

This is not a [dark factory](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/) where nobody reads the code. Split find, judge, fix, and repair so none of them can grade their own homework. Put the incentives where the agent can't edit them. Optimize for signal, not throughput. Leave a human on the doors that actually matter.

Until it was producing work at a peer level, it wasn't worth the time. That's the whole thing. The same standard I wrote about in [*Albert Einstein Walks Into a Library*](/writing/albert-einstein-library) — repeatable, accurate, auditable in itself — applied to the factory that ships the product.
